Everything on this site is meant to be traceable. This page says where the square-note notation comes from, how each chant was matched to its day, who wrote the English translations, and what the “About this chant” notes are built from.

The chant notation

The notation is drawn from the GregoBase chant database, a long-running community transcription of the printed chant books. GregoBase releases its chant transcriptions under CC0, a public-domain dedication, and none of the chants used here carries a copyright flag in that database.

Where a choice exists, this project takes the transcription made from the Graduale Romanum, most often the 1961 Solesmes edition. A few chants have never been transcribed from a Solesmes engraving; for those, the Editio Vaticana, the Vatican edition the Solesmes books themselves elaborate, is used instead. That substitution is always recorded on the chant, never made silently.

Every chant on the site carries a Source line naming the book and the database entry it was transcribed from, so any score here can be checked against the original. It reads like this:

Graduale Romanum (GregoBase #149)

When the Vatican edition stood in for a missing Solesmes engraving, the same line says so.

Which chant belongs to which day

The modern calendar and the 1962 calendar do not always use the same chants. Three references are used to determine which chant is used for which calendar:

  • Divinum Officium, whose transcriptions of the Missal supply the saints’ days and the Common of Saints for both calendars.
  • gregorien.info, hosted by the Diocese of Rottenburg-Stuttgart, for the temporal cycle from Advent through Christ the King on the modern calendar.
  • the Diocese of La Crosse sacred music resources, a cross-check for matching days to their proper Introits.

On the modern calendar, some Sundays in Ordinary Time sing different propers depending on which year of the three-year lectionary cycle it is. Those days carry all three settings, and the site picks the one belonging to the current year.

Both calendars deliberately sing one chant on more than one day where the Graduale itself repeats the text. That is the chant books’ own practice, not a gap here.

The English translations

The English beneath each sung chant is an original rendering, made for this project, in the register of the Douay-Rheims. It is deliberately not taken from the Roman Missal (ICEL) or the Gregorian Missal (Solesmes): those translations are under copyright and are not reproduced anywhere on this site.

These renderings were drafted with the help of a language model. It worked from the Latin actually being sung rather than from anything it had memorized, and each draft was then checked against two public-domain texts, the Douay-Rheims in the Challoner revision on the English side and the Clementine Vulgate on the Latin side in the Clementine Text Project’s digitization. Drafts that did not answer to the Latin were discarded.

The spoken prayers

On the 1962 calendar the site also carries the Mass’s spoken propers, the Collect, the Secret and the Postcommunion, for 177 Masses. Their Latin is taken verbatim from Divinum Officium’s transcription of the 1962 Missal, with two deliberate omissions: the Trinitarian formula that closes nearly every prayer, which is boilerplate rather than anything proper to the day, and the secondary commemoration of another saint or Sunday that older rubrics fold into some Masses, since the site shows one set of prayers per Mass.

The English follows the same practice as the sung texts. It is an original rendering in a traditional-missal register, drafted with the help of a language model working from the Latin rather than from anything it had memorized, and checked back against that Latin, with drafts that did not answer to it discarded. Where a prayer’s Latin is word for word one already translated elsewhere on the site, the same English is reused; prayers that differ even by a word or two are translated afresh rather than assumed identical.

The modern calendar’s spoken prayers are not included. The modern Missal is a current edition under copyright, in its Latin as well as in the ICEL English in common use, and is not reproduced here.

Scripture references and psalm numbering

Most of these chants are Scripture, and the site cites the passage each one draws on. The Psalms in particular account for a very large share of the repertoire: Introits, Graduals, Offertories and Communions are overwhelmingly psalm verses, so how the Psalter is numbered comes up constantly.

Citations here use modern psalm numbering, the convention of contemporary English Bibles. The Latin tradition numbers the Psalter differently, and the two drift apart by a psalm through most of the book, so where they diverge the citation names the Vulgate number as well, for example Ps. 45:3 (Vulgate numbering Ps. 44:3).

The “About this chant” notes

Every chant in the corpus carries a short note on what it is and where it comes from, with its own Source line beneath it. Those notes are built from three kinds of evidence:

  • the chant’s own Latin text and the Scripture it sets;
  • the full Mass formulary for the day it belongs to, the readings and the collect, by way of Divinum Officium;
  • background such as a feast’s history or a famous polyphonic setting, from Wikipedia.

These notes were written with the help of a language model. The evidence was gathered and verified first; the model was given that evidence and asked to put it into prose. It was not asked what it knew, it supplies no facts of its own, and it never writes a citation. Proposed drafts were checked against the evidence given, and drafts that were not properly based on the evidence were discarded.

How the page works

This is a plain web page. There is no account, no tracking, and nothing to install; everything happens in your browser.

  • The notation is drawn by Exsurge, an open-source square-note engraver (MIT-licensed, © 2016 Fr. Matthew Spencer, OSJ).
  • Playback is synthesized, a simple generated tone rather than a recording, which follows the score and highlights each syllable as it sounds.
  • The liturgical calendar is computed as you load the page, for both the modern General Roman Calendar and the 1962 calendar. No third-party calendar data is bundled.

Rights

The chant melodies are public domain; they are centuries older than copyright. The notation files come from GregoBase under CC0 and are cited chant by chant. Published choir recordings, Solesmes and others, are copyrighted, and none are bundled here; that is why playback is synthesized. The copyrighted ICEL and Solesmes English translations are not reproduced.

Found a mistake?

Corrections are genuinely welcome: a wrong note, a typo in the Latin, a translation that misses, a chant assigned to the wrong day. Open an issue on GitHub and say which day or feast you were looking at, what looks wrong, and what you expected instead. If you are comparing against a particular book or recording, naming it helps a great deal.
